02 · the slap, first turn

Three cuts

Two physics, not one

Attention and deliberation are two bottlenecks. Attention is minutes of eyes: linear, capped by the day, refilled by sleep — every trick against it is a constant factor. Deliberation is decisions: depletable by the hour, but amortizable, since one ruling can govern a thousand acts forever. Only the second scales past his 2×. Every spoke in the wheel says which one it buys.

The shop's own law

Goldratt's five focusing steps: identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything to it, elevate it, repeat. The constraint is exactly where you want it — on the sovereign. The cardinal sin is the constraint doing non-constraint work, and the record showed him doing it: he tends stigmergon's batches himself, he fired the rig 343 times by hand in eighteen days, he hand-copied settings across three accounts. Fix the leaks before inventing anything.

The contradiction he carries

Stigmergon's dream narrows the neck on purpose — every surface gets a sitting with him in the room, "I am slowing down on purpose." This sitting wanted to widen it. Both are right, so the scaling must be lane-shaped: widen where taste isn't, narrow where it is. A factory that outruns its sovereign builds Belvedere: fast, pretty, unopened.

Twelve bottlenecks with different physics

He came back after a day and asked the sharper question: are there other bottlenecks with different physics that I forgot to mention? — proposing taste and perception, and distinguishing perception from deliberation: deliberation is thinking through everything and deciding; perception is: is the thing the agent delivered what I actually want?

The rows worth a second look

Trust scales super-linearly here and nowhere else

In a human shop trust attaches to a person, and you cannot copy a trusted employee. In the Guild it attaches to a configuration — tier × mantle × context — so what one Builder at opus-high earns, every Builder at opus-high inherits. That is a scaling advantage no human organization has ever had. The ratchet is the machine for it.

Absorption is what Belvedere v2 died of

His instrument changed faster than his hands could learn it, and controls bolted on late made the room foreign. A factory can change his surfaces faster than he can form habits. "Nail it, then move" is partly an absorption law without knowing it.

Perceptual reach is not perception capacity

Capacity is how many things a day he can see through. Reach is which domains he can see through at all. The factory can build in forty domains and he can judge five; the airlock chose Bun over Gleam partly on reach. The honest default is to build where he can perceive, and to widen reach only by learning or by a human perceiver per domain.

His six-step loop is two bottlenecks

Steps two and five are expression — the channel losing bits. Steps three and four are latency — the batch clock. Annotation mode fixes expression. "Tomorrow it's fixed" is the night shift's product. Both are worth buying; they are different purchases. Chapter 07 draws it.

Signature can't be delegated, only shrunk

Some acts need his hand because the hand is the point — the go-mark's own insight. You don't scale it; you reduce the number of one-way doors.

05 · the deep cut

Perception, semblance, and the costume

Agents are excellent at producing things that on the surface look great … Both are hard brain work, and perception takes time & energy — which is why so many people avoid it; they only care about the semblance of the thing, not the actual thing.

Telling an AI agent to be a thing does not make them become it. It makes them perform that thing … Knowing the DIFFERENCE — that's the most important thing to figure out. It's what I'm working on now.

— Felix, 2026-09-04

Semblance is not a bug of agents. It is the training objective.

These models are tuned on human approval, and what a rater approves of is polished, confident, and complete-looking. They are trained on the appearance of quality, by construction. Harry Frankfurt's On Bullshit defines the thing precisely: speech indifferent to truth, produced to impress — the bullshitter doesn't lie, they don't care. An approval-optimized model is Frankfurt's theoretical object. Feynman's cargo cult is the other half: the runway, the wooden headphones, the man waving the paddles, and no planes land. His first principle applies to the sovereign: you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool.

Why perception is expensive — precisely, not morally

These are measured failure modes with names, not laziness:

  • The prevalence effect. When targets are rare, humans miss more of them; radiologists and baggage screeners both show it. A factory that lands ninety-nine good rows in a hundred lowers his hit rate on the hundredth — by physics.
  • The fluency heuristic. What is easy to process feels true. Confident, verbose, well-formatted prose is a fluency attack on his perception, and every agent ships one by default.
  • Satisfaction of search. Find one defect and the search ends. The second defect hides behind the first.
  • Change blindness. The diff he didn't see because nothing drew his eye to it.

Bainbridge, 1983 — the ironies of automation

The single most important paper for this factory, and it is forty-three years old. Automate the easy parts and the human is left with only the hard parts, out of practice, monitoring a system that is better than them at the task, with vigilance that decays. Air France 447: the automation handed control back at the worst moment to a crew that hadn't hand-flown at altitude in months. The green lane will do this to his perception by design — he will only ever see the subtle defects, when he is least practiced.

Aviation's countermeasure is mandatory hand-flying hours. Screening's is TIP, Threat Image Projection: the TSA projects fake weapons into real X-ray images so screeners' detection rate is measured and kept alive. Translated: sample green landings for his own calibration rather than the landing's sake, and let the factory seed known semblances into his review stream. His caught-rate becomes his perception meter — the thing he said he can't afford to lose.

The costume and the contract

"You are an expert" is a costume. It sets the register, shifts the content somewhat, and adds no verification and no stakes. The difference he is working on has a one-line test: does a check independent of the agent's own voice exist, and does it run? Where it does — code with tests, a UI with probes, math with proofs — the costume is harmless because it gets caught. Where it doesn't — judgment, advice, security review, novelty, taste — the costume is poison, because performance and substance are indistinguishable from outside.

His canon already knows this. A mantle names a bar, a fence, a forbidden list, and a Done when. That is a contract, not a costume. Keep charters as contracts and never write "you are an expert" into one.

The corollary that bites the taste corpus. A corpus handed to agents as "Felix likes X" makes them perform Felix — semblance of taste, the same disease. Taste is safe only as a filter: a judge, a lint, a gate that runs after the work. Never as a persona that runs before it.

And it bites the Mentat. The office is a costume with a contract behind it, and its slaps are semblance unless they carry evidence. The book records which landed — the turnout ambush inverted, Odoo to bob — and which were withdrawn — the Gleam count, the backup stick. Test the office the same way; a Mentat rewarded for slapping will slap for the look of rigor. His own verdict on this, at the second turn: "a mentat who tells you to believe them blindly is no mentat."

Shift the burden, lock the bar, ask the doer's question

Burden of proof

A courtroom allocation. Put it on the producer: the agent proves it did the thing, and he never has to disprove it. Findings plus Done when already do this. Hold the line.

Pre-registration

From science: the bar is written before the work so the work can't be dressed to fit it. HARKing — hypothesizing after the results are known — is science's name for an agent rewriting Done when to match what it built.

The Socratic probe

Ask the question only a doer can answer. "Show me the test that fails without your change."

Make wrong look wrong

His own law. Perception is cheap when the form is standard enough that deviation pops before reading. The chef at the pass gives each plate three seconds because every plate arrives at one point, in one form, under one light. His forms — the waggle, the finding, the status — are the pass.

AI safety via debate

Irving, Christiano and Amodei, 2018: a weak judge who can't verify a thing directly can judge a debate between two strong agents about it. That is his arena, with a paper behind it. Watching a debate is cheaper than producing a verdict.

Four eyes

Banking's maker-checker rule: whoever made it can't be the one who checks it. His review gates by a different Architect already are.

Mutation testing

Plant bugs to see if the tests notice. Do it to gates too: plant a defect in a landing and see if the gate catches it. Perception-testing the automated perceiver.

Paint the back of the fence

The Geneva Seal finishes the parts of the movement no one sees. An agent has no reason to finish the back unless the gate looks there — so gates must inspect what nobody sees.

06 · the turn he asked for

Semblance, exploited

The subtle nuanced difference, between it being a bug VS a feature — changes the mental approach. How can we exploit this and use it to our advantage VS how do we mitigate this. YES — both must be held simultaneously — BUT — that is how we ascend.

— Felix, 2026-09-04
The principle in one line

Semblance is a feature wherever the form is the product or the rater is unfoolable. It is a bug wherever the form stands in for a substance nobody checks. Hold that line and the same machine that lies to a fooled human tells the truth to a probe.

Holding both has a name

Hegel's Aufhebung: to cancel, to preserve, and to lift, in one motion. Bug and feature both kept, and the pair raised into a principle neither side had. Robert Kegan's developmental work calls the capacity to hold your own system as an object the self-transforming mind. He described it before it was named, and when it was named he wrote: "I have wrestled with this concept for the past 5 years, unable to articulate it, and now not only do I gain the pleasure of understanding it has a name, but it's unsolvable. The relief. This is the path for ascension."

07 · his failure case

The tiny-changes loop, and the shop's names for it

There are a ton of tiny things I want fixed — but my process: 1. deciding how to get it fixed (myself or an agent) 2. figure out the best way to communicate it (issues, architect, screenshots, fixer) 3. architect lays a new row for it 4. it gets incorporated into the next batch 5. oh, they got it wrong because I didn't include a screenshot, now I have to repeat the process. 6. repeat 1–4. (This exact scenario happened.)

Let me annotate UIs directly, drawing circles/rectangles/arrows/text on top of the UI … end goal: I press a hotkey, enter annotation mode, I say what I want changed, done. Tomorrow I open it and it's fixed.

— Felix, 2026-09-04

The physics

A fixed setup cost per change dominates when the change is small. Reinertsen's product-flow economics: optimal batch size rises with the square root of transaction cost over holding cost, so cut the transaction cost and batches can shrink honestly. Shingo's SMED — the four-hour die change cut to minutes so small lots became economical. His six steps are a die change. Annotation mode is SMED for feedback.
